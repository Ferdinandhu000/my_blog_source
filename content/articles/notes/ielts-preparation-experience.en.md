## My Background

To start with, I did have a reasonably solid foundation in English: **134** in the Zhejiang college entrance exam, **625** in CET-4, and **656** in CET-6. I also spent my entire first year at university taking language classes and regularly attended courses taught entirely in English. Still, writing and speaking had always been my weaker areas, so those were my main priorities during preparation.

## My Preparation Journey

First, here is my result:

![My IELTS test result](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Ferdinandhu000/my_blog_img/master/20261024193227.png)

Getting this result on my first attempt was a pleasant surprise, especially because the journey to the test was full of unexpected turns. I had originally booked the October 11 session, but it was suddenly cancelled, so I moved my booking to the following week. Just a few days before the rescheduled test, I was told that my test fee would be refunded in full. Essentially, I got to take the test for free. Two days before the exam, I learned that my speaking test would start at 8 a.m., which meant changing my train ticket and booking a hotel that same night so I could arrive in the test city earlier. Looking back, this actually worked out well: I had somewhere to settle in instead of rushing off a high-speed train on test day, and I could return to the hotel to rest after the speaking test.

I had planned to prepare over the summer, but I had too much going on and did not feel much urgency. I had not even booked the test yet. In practice, I did very little beyond learning how the test worked, collecting some materials, and completing one or two sets of Cambridge IELTS practice tests. Before I knew it, September and the new semester had arrived.

I returned to university a week early to study. After weighing up my university commitments, I settled on taking the test the week after the National Day holiday, which gave me a little over a month of serious preparation. Research commitments meant that I still did not study particularly consistently during the first two weeks. I think I was even considering submissions to ICASSP and ICLR, but I eventually let those go when I realized that meeting those deadlines would leave almost no time for IELTS. It was only on September 23 that I fully committed to exam preparation. By then, I had worked through roughly Cambridge IELTS 15 to 17 and practised some speaking questions from a question bank, but I had barely started writing practice.

The following weeks, through the end of the National Day holiday, were both the most intense and the most stressful. Being told that my original test had been cancelled just one day before it was due to happen made things even harder. I remember receiving the text message during a lecture and barely taking in anything afterward. Instead, I spent much of the time talking to a friend who had taken IELTS over the summer. He had received structured IELTS training and knew many things that were unfamiliar to me as a self-taught candidate. That was quite a shock: it made me question how effective my preparation had actually been. Embarrassingly, that was when I first learned that listening gap-fill questions could say `NO MORE THAN TWO/THREE WORDS`! All the questions I had practised in Cambridge IELTS 15–20 had used `ONE WORD ONLY`. I kept wondering that evening what score I would have received if the test had gone ahead as planned. It was a difficult period.

Fortunately, much of my friend's advice was practical and helpful. Following his suggestion about improving my writing, I booked two lessons with an IELTS writing teacher. There was no magical moment when everything suddenly clicked or my writing improved overnight. Most of the lessons involved discussing problems I had encountered in my own writing. What mattered more was that those conversations gave me a much-needed boost in confidence, which I will come back to below.

By the final few days before the actual test, I felt much less anxious than I had the previous week, before the original test date. I kept practising listening and reviewing my listening and writing notes. One thing I have not mentioned yet is my usual listening and reading performance. Although my actual test scores in those sections were very high, I had never scored 8.5 in any practice test or mock exam. I had only reached 8 once or twice; most of my results were around 7 or 7.5. Even just before the test, I could still get five or six consecutive answers wrong on a difficult listening gap-fill exercise. What I want to say is this: **that is normal! You do not know what you are capable of until you are in the exam room and fully focused. Have confidence in yourself.**

On test day, it was very windy in Dalian. Luckily, the hotel was close to the test centre. With my speaking test scheduled for 8 a.m., I got up shortly after six and watched some speaking mock tests on YouTube to get into the rhythm of a natural conversation. Then I reviewed my own speaking notes and tried answering a few questions. Once that felt comfortable, I headed to the test centre. The wait before the speaking test felt long, but the test itself went by incredibly quickly. My examiner was a Black guy, and by the end I almost wished we could have kept chatting, haha. I left with a pencil as a souvenir and got ready for the afternoon's written tests.

The afternoon test environment was excellent. There were partitions on both sides, the lighting was just right, and the supplied 3M earplugs worked very well. It was easy to get fully absorbed in the tasks. Doing listening and reading in the real exam felt quite different from practising at home. For listening, I usually skipped ahead through the introductory audio and pauses at the beginning and end of practice recordings. In the actual test, those intervals gave me extra time to read the questions, especially the multiple-choice questions that require more preparation. I felt I could match almost every question to a specific part of the recording, with very few answers left ambiguous. Writing, of course, was still a race against the clock. My Task 1 question was a static chart, the type I felt least comfortable with because most of my practice had involved charts showing changes over time. Task 2 was about education, a topic I knew reasonably well, so that was a relief. I wrote 200 words for Task 1 and 360 for Task 2, but had no time to check everything from beginning to end. I corrected a few grammar errors, and then the countdown ended.

## What I Learned

**The most important thing is confidence. Confidence. Confidence!!!**

There are several ways this matters. If you are studying independently rather than taking a course, **have confidence in your ability to learn on your own**. So many materials and tutorials are available online now. To me, much of what an intensive IELTS course provides is structure and supervision. Your own self-discipline and persistence still matter enormously.

**Confidence matters in the speaking test too.** I once came across the idea that IELTS speaking is about more than language ability alone: it also tests how you organize your thoughts and communicate. You can express yourself well with simple vocabulary and sentence structures. In my own test, I mostly used words I had learned in primary or middle school. During speaking practice, the biggest obstacle was rarely a lack of sophisticated vocabulary or a word I could not remember. More often, I simply did not know what to say about the question itself. Sometimes, I would have struggled to give a good answer even in Chinese. What music do you like? What is your favourite colour? What is your favourite book? Tell me about a hobby. Many of us rarely stop to think deeply about these questions in everyday life, let alone answer them in English.

My suggestion is to spend some time thinking about these apparently simple questions during your preparation, and to rely a little less on internet catchphrases in your everyday conversations. For example, if someone asks, _What would you do if you saw rubbish on the street?_, try not to stop at the equivalent of, “Well, obviously I'd pick it up and throw it away.” That may be how you would chat casually with a friend, but it does not give you much to develop in the test. As with writing, you need a connected train of thought. Even if you start with a very ordinary idea, try to develop it enough for the other person to understand it and want to ask more. Practising how you think and talk about everyday questions can help you have something to say in the exam. Here is a [video I recommend](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1L44y197J2/?spm_id_from=333.337.search-card.all.click&vd_source=859b43f788d3bd90fb79d1040d3ce28f).

**You also need confidence in your writing.** If you are studying on your own, AI may be your main source of writing feedback. Before my two lessons with the writing teacher, I relied entirely on AI to review my essays. If you have tried this too, you may have noticed how low the scores can be. At first, they seem almost unbelievable; after a while, though, you start thinking, “Perhaps that really is my level.” I am not saying AI feedback is useless. I am saying you should **evaluate it critically**. Almost none of my AI-reviewed essays reached 7. A few Task 1 responses did, but most scores were 6 or 6.5, and 5.5 was common when I first started.

I also noticed something strange. Writing is assessed across four criteria: **Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, Task Achievement / Task Response, and Coherence and Cohesion**. In my experience, AI seemed especially harsh on grammar. My average score for that criterion was only around 5.5–6. I certainly made grammatical mistakes, but the relationship between the number of errors and the final score often felt overly strict and mechanical. Once, I corrected the grammar errors in an essay and **submitted it to the AI again**. The grammar score jumped from 5.5 to 6.5, and the other three criteria rose slightly as well. That made me feel that surface-level language errors could disproportionately influence AI feedback. My impression is that a human examiner may not treat every small grammatical slip as quite so decisive. I came to place more emphasis on developing arguments and connecting ideas—TA/TR and CC—rather than getting stuck on an AI-generated score. Becoming too fixated on those scores can undermine your confidence and distract you from those broader writing issues. Of course, grammar errors are difficult to eliminate completely under time pressure. What you can do is leave a little time to check obvious spelling mistakes, singular and plural forms, tenses, and common collocation errors.

**On vocabulary:** I did not set aside time to memorize IELTS word lists during my preparation, although this will vary from person to person. After completing a few reading passages, I found that unfamiliar vocabulary rarely prevented me from understanding the text. Unfamiliar technical terms often came with an explanation in English. Personally, I sometimes felt that the word-selection gap-fill questions in CET-6 demanded an even larger vocabulary, haha.

**On practice tests:** My strongest recommendation is **not to rely entirely on Cambridge IELTS, and especially not only on newer volumes such as 15–20**. Some older tests use question formats that differ from the newer ones, and you may encounter those formats in the actual exam. The listening questions allowing `TWO/THREE WORDS` are one example worth preparing for. I was fortunate that my own listening test only used `ONE WORD` answers and that the reading question types were familiar, but I would not recommend counting on that. After working through newer tests, look at older question formats too. I also recommend this [IELTS practice website](https://www.9fnnn.com/). The questions are said to come from actual test sittings, although that is how they are described rather than something I independently verified. I only discovered the site two days before my test and did a small amount of practice, but the experience felt fairly similar to the real exam. As for its “trending” lists and “likelihood of appearing” predictions, I would take those lightly. There is a selection-bias element here: with so many people taking IELTS, frequent reports of a question do not necessarily mean you personally have a high chance of seeing it. Still, I found the questions useful for practice and for getting familiar with a broader range of material.

**On speaking:** I do not feel particularly qualified to give extensive advice here, since my speaking score was not especially high. I did not work with a speaking teacher. Instead, I usually chatted with ChatGPT and went through the current question bank, including retained questions and classic topics. Reflection afterward matters too. I kept my speaking practice, along with my writing practice, in Obsidian so an AI agent could read and edit the notes. After each conversation, for example, I would ask the AI to export the discussion and save it in my notes. Once enough material had accumulated, I could organize it by theme and find ways to reuse my own experiences across different topics. I found this very helpful. Still, avoid depending entirely on AI responses, especially its suggested speaking answers: they can be **very mechanical and overly formal**, with little sense of a real conversation. I recommend watching [薛麦 Max's Part 1–3 videos on Bilibili](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1CheP6TEb5/?spm_id_from=333.1387.homepage.video_card.click&vd_source=859b43f788d3bd90fb79d1040d3ce28f) to learn from her expressions and approach to answering. I also enjoyed watching [IELTS speaking mock tests on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@Ieltsadvantage), both to learn from native speakers' expressions and to see what candidates at different score levels actually sound like. That can help you get a rough sense of your own speaking level.

A special thank-you to my girlfriend, who scored an impressive 7 on her first IELTS attempt. I am also grateful to my writing teacher, who gave me a new perspective on IELTS writing in the final week before the test and, more importantly, helped me regain my confidence. [Speaking ideas video on Bilibili](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1L44y197J2/): A reference for the discussion above about developing a complete answer to a simple speaking question.

[薛麦 Max's speaking videos on Bilibili](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1CheP6TEb5/): Learn expressions and approaches to Part 1–3 answers, especially how to sound conversational.

[IELTS Advantage on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@Ieltsadvantage): Watch speaking mock tests featuring candidates at different score levels, or get into the rhythm of an English conversation before your test.
